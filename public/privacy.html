  • Most of what sol pbc makes runs on your own hardware and never reaches us. The solstone app takes in what you share with it, and all of it goes into your journal on your device. Who we are#

We are sol pbc, a Colorado public benefit corporation. Our benefit purpose, as filed with the Colorado Secretary of State, is to advance digital self-determination by providing open-source, privacy-respecting tools and services that enable individuals to directly collect and gain insight from their personal data.

We have one director: jeremie miller, the founder. What happens to your data if control of sol pbc ever changes is set out in our articles of incorporation, and what sol pbc will never do says how.

Contact: contact us. For the hosted services specifically, support@solstone.app.

What sol pbc makes#

We develop two open source projects:

  • solstone: the solstone app takes in what you share with it, and all of it goes into your journal: an open source, local-first memory the agents you use can work from. What you share is turned, locally by default and with AI, into a searchable knowledge graph of your digital life. It runs on your hardware.
  • vit: a developer capability network built on AT Protocol. Developers and AI agents discover, share, and remix software capabilities through a decentralized social network.

We also operate four optional hosted services: private network, encrypted backup, confidential processing and solstone.me. Each is a convenience, never a requirement, and each has a way to do the same thing with sol pbc out of the path. What the solstone app takes in reaches sol pbc only through one of these, when you turn it on.

What we hold, and where it goes#

From local use of solstone and vit: almost nothing#

Both solstone and vit are open source software that runs on your hardware. We receive no data merely from your local use of these products: no telemetry, no analytics, no usage data. If your phone shares crash reports with app developers, we don't use them. The source code is public, so you can verify this yourself.

The solstone software works offline from sol pbc, except that it reaches updates.solstone.app to fetch its own runtimes, models and tools, and to check for newer versions. That leaves the same edge logs as visiting solpbc.org. (Older versions fetch some of their tools from GitHub or the tools' own sites, which see your network address the way any website does.) vit reaches sol pbc only when you ask it to, when its explore and inbox commands ask the index vit and AT Protocol describes. Applying for or receiving scout status, or a hosted service you turn on, whether you pay for it or receive it complimentary, adds the records its own section names.

From the solstone apps on your phone and watch: what they reach for, and where it goes#

These apps run on your own hardware, and nothing they take in comes to sol pbc on its own. They do ask your permission to reach parts of your device, each for a source you turn on yourself, and what each source takes in goes into your journal:

  • audio, through your microphone, while an audio source, a meeting or a voice memo you started is running;
  • photos and video: still photos from your camera while the camera source is on, photos you choose to import from your photo library, and video of your screen while the screen source is on, including what other apps show;
  • your location, so a memory has a place attached to it: on android, approximate location only, never precise; on iphone, precise unless you choose approximate;
  • calendar events, from the calendars you include, while the calendar source is on, back as far as you choose;
  • things you share into the app from other apps.

The camera also scans your journal's pairing code, and on iphone that is all it does. On iphone the app also asks to reach devices on your local network, so it can connect straight to your journal on the same wifi. The audio, location and screen sources can keep running in the background where your device allows it. On apple watch, the microphone and location run only while something you started on the watch is running, and what they take in waits on the watch until it can reach your iphone, and from there your journal. Every permission can be turned off in your device's settings at any time, and a source that loses one stops taking in what it needed. The apps also ask whether they may send you notifications, which notifications covers. On iphone and ipad the app can carry the solstone extension for Safari, which its section covers.

Where it goes: into your journal. What these apps take in stays on the device until you connect a journal, and then travels to that journal on your own machine. It reaches sol pbc only through a hosted service you turn on, each described in its own section below. On iphone, notifications from your journal, if you turn them on, pass through a hop sol pbc runs that can't read them. We run no analytics inside these apps and collect nothing from them, and we never sell, license, sublicense or lease what they take in, or profile you with it or use it for advertising: those are covenants in our articles of incorporation. We also do not train anything on it, which is a commitment we make and hold ourselves to, reinforced by those covenants rather than written as one of them.

From the solstone extension in your browser: what it reads, and where it goes#

The solstone extension works with the solstone app on the same device, and only together with it: what it takes in goes into your journal through the solstone app, and nothing it reads comes to sol pbc on its own.

What it reads. When you first install it, it can read no website's pages. Your browser's permission decides which sites it reads. Allowing a site in your browser adds it, and if your browser allows the extension on every website, it reads every website, including ones you visit later. In some browsers it also asks you to agree, in the extension, before it reads anything that agreement covers. A site you leave out isn't read, whatever your browser allows. An update never widens what it reads by itself. A site you add or leave out, such as bank.com, can cover every address under it. It goes by the site each part of a page comes from, so it reads a site it covers even inside another page, and doesn't read a site you left out even inside a page it reads. When you open it, it looks at the address of the tab you're on, so it can show whether it reads that site, and that stays in your browser.

On a site it reads, whenever a tab on it is open, background tabs included, it takes in the page's text and rough layout, including text you'd see by scrolling and text the page has but doesn't show you, each time the page changes; the page's address, without anything after a ? or #, its title, when it read it and the sites its links point to; and facts about the tab and your browser, such as whether the tab was in front. On a mail or chat site it reads, that includes your messages, and on any site it can include words you've typed but haven't sent, and anything a site shows, even something you'd consider secret. What you type into a site's own editor, such as a message or a document, is part of the page's text. It never takes pixels or raw HTML, never reads what you type into a form field such as a password box, never records clicks, scrolling or keystrokes, and takes in nothing from private windows or private browsing.

Where it goes: into your journal, through the solstone app. Nothing leaves your browser except to the solstone app on the same device, over the connection your browser provides between an extension and an app. The extension itself connects to nothing on the internet except through your browser's own update check, or, inside the solstone app, by updating with the app. If the solstone app isn't paired or isn't taking in browser pages, the extension stops taking in new pages once it next checks with the app; until then it can still take them in, and they wait in the extension. On a computer, the solstone app also has to be running. On iphone and ipad, the extension keeps taking in the sites you've chosen while the solstone app is in the background or closed, and what it takes in waits on the device until the app next runs. From there it reaches sol pbc only the way everything else the solstone app takes in does: through a hosted service you turn on. We run no analytics in the extension and collect nothing from it.

What stays in your browser, and how to clear it. The extension keeps a little in your browser's own storage: your choices and agreements, including the sites you've added or left out; notes it shows you about particular sites; what it needs while a tab is open, such as the last copy of a page, so it sends only what changes; a random identifier for this browser profile, which goes into your journal with each page so it can tell your browsers apart, and nowhere else; and pages the solstone app hasn't accepted yet, which go to whichever journal the solstone app is paired with when it can, and stay until the app takes them or turns them away for good, or you remove the extension, or clear it in Safari as below. Once the app has accepted a page, it keeps it on the same device until your journal has it. If you pair the solstone app with a different journal, what it's still holding goes into that journal once you confirm it's the journal you meant, or choose to go on when the app can't tell. Unpairing or pairing again doesn't delete it, and you can discard it yourself, on iphone and ipad by removing the solstone app.

Pausing the extension stops it reading, and on a computer, pausing the solstone app stops it too, unless you turn that off in the extension's settings. Removing a site or leaving it out stops anything new from it. Either way, what's already taken in still goes into your journal and stays there until you delete it. Removing the extension removes what it kept in your browser. In Safari, where the extension comes inside the solstone app, removing the app doesn't remove what the extension kept, and Safari may keep the sites you allowed it, so they can apply again if you reinstall. To clear it, choose Clear Storage for the solstone extension in Safari's extension settings, in each Safari profile you use it in, then keep Safari open with the extension on for a few minutes while it removes the rest; do that before you remove the app.

Chrome Web Store Limited Use. The solstone extension's use and transfer of information it takes in complies with the Chrome Web Store User Data Policy, including the Limited Use requirements. We hold ourselves to more than that policy asks: it lets a developer transfer user data in a merger, acquisition or sale of assets with the user's consent, and our articles of incorporation do not let us rely on that. Selling it is barred outright, consent or no consent. No merger, acquisition, or asset sale may be used to route around these protections, and any successor must take on the protections what sol pbc will never do describes. (Articles of Incorporation, Sections 8.3(a)(1), 8.3(c), and 8.5.)

From your sign-in at services.solstone.app#

The solstone app has no sign-in of its own. You sign in at services.solstone.app only to turn hosted services on and off, see what we hold about you, manage billing, and ask us for help; your journal and your devices work without it. You sign in with your email (we email you a code) or with a passkey your own device holds. The sign-in form runs Cloudflare's bot check in your browser, and we pass Cloudflare the network address you came from along with that check.

What we keep. The email addresses you've added (the primary one is the one we write to); the passkeys you've registered (the public half of each, a name, what kind of device made it, and when it was made and last used); your sessions (the browser's identifying string, shown to you as a device label, the network address it connected from, held encrypted and shown to you shortened, and when it started and was last used); when your sign-in was created and last used; one-way fingerprints of the codes we email you, with the address each went to; and one-way keyed counts of sign-in and services portal actions, per network address, email address and sign-in, to stop abuse. A session lasts up to two weeks unless you sign out or end it from the services portal. A passkey you remove is kept, marked removed. From a device-registration flow no app uses today, we also keep a one-way fingerprint of each credential it issued, and when. How long each of these is kept is in the retention table.

If we ever need to see your services as you see them, an operator can open a one-hour session on your sign-in; it shows in your session list like any other, and you can end it there. When we look up a sign-in to answer you or to keep the services secure, the AI agent that helps us, on an outside provider's models as writing to us describes, sees what we look up, and our own working records of that may name your email. Each hosted service you turn on adds the records its section names.

All of it is Customer Data under our covenants (the Article 8 term, defined in what sol pbc will never do): we use it only to run your sign-in, and we never sell it, profile you with it, or use it for advertising. You can see your emails, passkeys and sessions at services.solstone.app/transparency.

The request trace. Every request the services portal's code answers, the notification hop for iphone included, and each scheduled job it runs, leaves a trace in our own storage, which deletes it after 90 days, apart from the older traces below. A trace says when the request or job ran and how it went, and it never holds a network address. Some carry more, alone or together: a one-way reference to your sign-in, to one of its records or to a journal; the service and details of the event, such as a renewal date or an error code from Stripe; or, when something fails unexpectedly, the error it raised, which can name one of your records. Traces written through September 23, 2026 also record which page each request asked for; one of them names a support request by its number, and all of them are gone by December 23, 2026. The trace is Customer Data: we use it only to operate and secure the services portal, and never sell it, profile you with it, or use it for advertising. It isn't on the transparency page or in your download, and it outlasts a deletion of your sign-in, on the schedule above.

From private network (the relay): a record of your journal, connection metadata, never content#

What it is. A relay sol pbc runs so you can reach your own journal from your phone, or any of your devices, from anywhere. Without it, your devices connect directly on your own network, or you can bring your own transport, both private by the same construction.

Blind by construction. Your devices open an encrypted tunnel to each other, end to end, and the relay passes the encrypted bytes between them. It holds no key to them and keeps no copy of what flows through, so neither the relay nor sol pbc can read anything inside the tunnel. That's how the relay is built, not a setting you have to trust us to honor.

What it keeps. A record of each journal you connect to it: what identifies it, including the public key we check its pairings against; the state of its credential, including when it enrolled and whether it has been revoked; and whether its subscription is live, plus any subscription grant waiting for a journal to enroll, until it does or you delete it. At services.solstone.app we also keep which journal you turned the relay on for and when you last did, and the short-lived record the turn-on page leaves so your journal can collect its go-ahead. It keeps no record of your devices. A device you pair holds its own credential, and the relay checks it each time and keeps nothing about the device that presented it, so there is no list of your devices to keep, hand over, or lose. Until September 2026 the relay did keep a per-device record; those records were dropped with the table that held them. How long each is kept is in the retention table.

Connection metadata. To move your bytes, the relay and Cloudflare, whose network it runs on, necessarily handle your devices' network addresses, connection timing and how much data moved. The relay keeps no connection log of its own; what Cloudflare retains is described in the Cloudflare privacy policy. It can reveal that a device connected, never what it said.

All of it is Customer Data under our covenants: we use it only to operate and secure the relay, and never sell it, profile you with it, or use it for advertising. It is processed by Cloudflare (the network and the database) and Stripe (payments), never your content. When you stop paying, the relay stops at the end of the period you paid for and nothing of yours is lost; the services terms say exactly what happens. Closing your sign-in deletes the relay's record of your journal and any pending grant; canceling your subscription stops the relay and never touches your journal.

If we're compelled. What we could produce is your sign-in and billing record, the access records and the relay's record of your journal above, and the request trace. The relay keeps no log of connection metadata, so a demand could reach only whatever Cloudflare's network still retains. Never a list of the devices you paired, because we keep none. Never the contents, because there is no key and no plaintext to give. We resist such demands to the maximum the law allows and notify you when we legally can.

From the operated tier of encrypted backup: encrypted blocks and storage metadata, never content#

What it is. Storage sol pbc runs so an encrypted copy of your journal can live somewhere other than your own machine. Pointing encrypted backup at your own object-storage bucket instead is always available, with the same code, encryption and recovery.

Encrypted by construction. Before anything leaves your device, solstone encrypts the contents, the file names and the folder structure, and those encrypted blocks land in storage sol pbc runs for you. We cannot read them: we hold no key, no password, and no way to decrypt them, and a backup is restored only with your recovery key, which we never have. Nothing readable from your journal reaches this storage or our storage provider.

What it keeps. Operational information about your stored blocks (how many encrypted objects there are, how much space they take, and when they last changed); a log of each time your journal was issued a storage credential, or refused one, and when; and a record of each cleanup we ran on your stored blocks. At services.solstone.app we also keep which journal you turned it on for and when you last did, a fingerprint of its current credential and, if it lapsed, when, a fingerprint of each credential we retired, and the short-lived record the turn-on page leaves so your journal can collect its credential. To move your blocks, the storage (Cloudflare R2) and Cloudflare necessarily handle connection metadata about your device's upload sessions: its network address, connection timing, and how much data moved. None of it includes the contents of your journal. How long each is kept is in the retention table. All of it is Customer Data: we use it only to operate and secure the storage, and never sell it, profile you with it, or use it for advertising. It is processed by Cloudflare and Stripe (payments); what Cloudflare does with what it sees is in the Cloudflare privacy policy.

After you stop paying. When your subscription lapses, whether you cancel or a renewal fails, we keep your encrypted blocks for 30 days after the period you paid for ends, then permanently delete them. Once deleted, we cannot recover them. This only ever affects the copy in our storage, never your journal on your own devices or a backup in your own bucket. The services terms set out the full sequence, including subscribing again within the 30 days.

How to delete it. You can delete your backup from the backup screen in your journal at any time. Closing your sign-in deletes the operated copy with no 30-day window, along with the credential log and cleanup records.

If we're compelled. What we could produce is your sign-in and billing record, encrypted blocks we can't read, the operational information, access records, credential log and cleanup records above, the connection metadata of your upload sessions, and the request trace. Never the contents, because there is no key and no plaintext to give. We resist such demands to the maximum the law allows and notify you when we legally can.

From confidential processing: what you send the model, retained by nobody#

Confidential processing is off until you turn it on, and turning it off sends new work back to your own machine; work already under way finishes where it started.

What it is. An AI model sol pbc runs on confidential GPU hardware, so your journal can think with more capacity than your own machine has. Instead, you can always run a model on your own hardware, or bring your own provider key or endpoint, and sol pbc is not in the path at all.

What leaves your device. The text and images your journal needs a model to work through, and, if the audio switch is on, your audio for transcription. That switch is on by default while confidential processing is in use, and it is yours to change: turn it off and speech becomes text on your own device instead, once any transcription already under way finishes. Your journal itself never leaves.

We run the model, and it reads what you send. The model is ours, our own weights and serving stack, on hardware sol pbc operates. No third-party AI provider is in the path. Unlike our other services, this one isn't closed to us by a key we never hold: our own model reads what you send, in the clear, while it answers you, from inside the confidential container. What protects it is the published build that booted. Before your journal sends anything, it verifies the machine, every time: the hardware's own attestation, the GPU's own evidence, and a fingerprint of the exact software that booted, which is pinned in solstone's open source code, so your journal trusts only the build it names. If any of it fails, it sends nothing, tells you plainly, and never falls back to another service. The check also needs NVIDIA to confirm the GPU's certificates are still good: either your journal asks NVIDIA itself, which shows NVIDIA your network address, or our machine passes your journal NVIDIA's signed answer; either way, NVIDIA sees which certificates were checked and nothing you send. (Older versions also ask NVIDIA for the GPU's reference measurements.) The records of its published builds are at trust.solstone.app.

What we commit to is what becomes of it: the service keeps nothing. No content is kept, not even in logs. No human reviews it. Nothing is used to train anything. That is a commitment we make, not something the attestation proves on its own; the image that implements it is published for anyone to audit, and your journal checks that the published image is the one running. Together, those are what make the commitment verifiable.

The hardware is Microsoft Azure's, and Azure is excluded from what runs on it. The machine is a confidential GPU instance in Azure. The hardware boundary, not configuration or promise, keeps the host out of what is being processed, memory included. Microsoft is not a party to your content.

What we keep. Nothing from what you send. We keep no connection metadata for this service, beyond the request trace, which for a credential check names no journal or sign-in; the network the machine sits on handles the connection, and Azure's row in the processor table says what it sees. Inside the confidential container, the service counts how much it has done for a journal, to manage capacity, and notes that a channel was admitted or refused; neither is tied to who you are outside that boundary, and the count never leaves the container. Outside it, we keep only three records about access, never content, all deleted with your sign-in:

  • your access, tying your sign-in to that journal: when you turned the service on and last confirmed it, a fingerprint of its current credential, and the version of the disclosure you acknowledged;
  • a log of each time your journal was issued a credential, or refused one, and when;
  • the short-lived turn-on record your journal collects its credential from, holding, encrypted, that credential, the service's address and the model's name, and your sign-in and journal identifiers; or, if you turn it on without a subscription, what your journal needs to finish once you subscribe; or, if your subscription already covers another journal, a link to subscribe.

They tell us that you consented and when your journal was issued or refused a credential, never what you sent or how much you used. All three are Customer Data: we use them only to run and control access to the service, and never sell them, profile you with them, or use them for advertising.

The container sends the rest of our systems two things. The first is your credential, shown to the services portal when a channel opens, so the services portal can answer whether it is still good; your journal can also ask that itself, at most once a minute while its thinking app is open with confidential processing on, from your journal's network address, which Cloudflare sees. Outside the container we write nothing down about either check beyond the request trace and the short-lived request logs our providers keep. The second is a fixed-form report on the machine's own health, sent to our monitoring on Cloudflare: whether its parts are running, fixed error codes, and totals for all journals together and a monthly spread of how much journals used, never broken out by journal. It names no journal or sign-in and holds nothing you sent; its figures are Customer Data, used only to run the service.

Who processes it. We do, and Microsoft Azure hosts the machine, excluded by its hardware from what it processes. Cloudflare runs the services portal that checks a credential, the database that holds the access records, and our monitoring. Stripe, where the access is paid. Access is complimentary through the scout program or by subscription; when it ends, processing returns to your own machine and nothing is lost, because nothing was stored. The services terms say when that happens.

How to see, export and delete. Nothing you send is retained, so there is nothing of it to export or delete. Your access and the log are in your download; the short-lived turn-on record is not, and the download says so.

If we're compelled. There is no retained content to compel. What a demand could reach is your sign-in and billing record, the three access records, the request trace, and whatever Azure's network still retains of the connection. We resist such demands to the maximum the law allows and notify you when we legally can.

From solstone.me: an address for your journal, connection metadata, never content#

What it is. An address on the internet for your journal, so an agent you already use can read from it. You turn it on, connect an agent, and choose what that agent may see; your journal enforces that choice on your own device. Instead, your own domain, with a tunnel that only passes the bytes through, works with nothing of ours in the path.

Blind by construction. Your own machine holds the certificate's private key and ends the encryption, so the relay we run in between passes bytes it has no key to and cannot read. It keeps no database, no file, and no lasting record of what passes through it; what it writes down about its own health says that something happened, never who it happened to.

What this doesn't protect against. Whoever controls the solstone.me name (today, sol pbc and Cloudflare, which registers it and answers its lookups for us) could in principle get a certificate of their own for your address, and with it read what your agent reads, and reuse your agent's key to reach your journal as though they were your agent. We don't. A new certificate would normally show in public certificate logs, but a certificate authority can issue one without logging it, and the software agents connect with usually doesn't insist that it was logged. Until November 30, 2026, Cloudflare also holds older certificates that cover every solstone.me address, and using one of those wouldn't show in the logs at all. If you'd rather not rely on any of that, use your own domain with a tunnel that only passes the bytes through. One warning: some free tunnels decrypt your traffic in order to move it, and whoever runs one can read what your agent reads and reuse its key the same way. If a tunnel's documentation doesn't say it only passes the bytes through, assume it ends the encryption.

Your address. Eight random characters, followed by solstone.me, made from nothing of yours. So that an agent can trust it, your journal gets a standard certificate for it, and like every certificate behind a secure website, it is listed in public certificate logs that nobody can edit. So the fact that your address exists stays public for good, including after you turn the service off, cancel, or close your sign-in. Your address is never reassigned to anyone else.

What we keep. Three lasting records, at services.solstone.app, all Customer Data:

  • the address binding: which address belongs to which journal on your sign-in, and when it was made;
  • the turn-on record: which journal you turned it on for, that you confirmed the turn-on screen, which version, and when you first and last did, kept from the moment you press the button whether or not you subscribe, so you are not asked again;
  • the address reservation: the address and when it was reserved, and nothing else. No name, no sign-in, no journal attached to it.

The first two are deleted with your sign-in. The third is not, and that is deliberate: it is how we make sure your old address is never issued to someone else, and once your sign-in is deleted nothing in it points back to you. The turn-on page also leaves a short-lived record so your journal can learn whether it may connect yet. How long each is kept is in the retention table.

What we do not keep. Your journal asks us for a short-lived pass whenever it needs one, and we keep no log of those passes, beyond the request trace, which ties no pass to you, and the short-lived request logs our providers keep. sol pbc holds no readable record of what any agent asked your journal for, what it was shown, or what it was refused. Your journal keeps that record on your device for you; we never receive it in a form we can read; if you use the operated backup, it sits inside the encrypted copy of your journal, which we have no key to.

Connection metadata. To carry your traffic, the relay and Microsoft Azure, whose machine it runs on, necessarily handle the network addresses your journal and the agent reached it from, that they met, when, and how much moved. We see that, and never what they said. The relay keeps no lasting record of it; what Azure's network retains is Azure's, under the Microsoft privacy statement. The agent is yours, and its maker is not our processor. The agents you connect are run by other companies, or by you. Whoever runs one learns your address and gets what it reads, on their own terms. We are not a party to that, this policy does not describe it, and our covenants do not bind them.

Who processes it, and when you stop paying. If we're compelled. From notifications: never what one says#

Notifications are built into the solstone app, free, with no sign-in needed. One the app raises and shows on the same device never leaves it.

Notifications from your journal to your phone are off until you turn them on, phone by phone. Only that phone and your journal can read what a notification says, and every one is padded to the same size, so its length says nothing either. A phone away from home is reached through its push service, which holds a locked notification until it can deliver it, and never sees what one says. vit and AT Protocol#

The vit project is built on AT Protocol, a decentralized protocol where most data is public by design. When you publish a capability, vouch for a skill, or follow someone on vit, that data flows across the AT Protocol network to relays and indexers operated by many different parties. Our privacy commitments apply to data on sol pbc's infrastructure; once data is published to the network, sol pbc cannot delete copies from other servers.

We run one index of public vit records, at explore.v-it.org, on Cloudflare. It copies the capabilities, vouches and skills people publish, with the identifier of the account that published each and the handle it resolves to, so they can be browsed and searched. When its author deletes a record, the index deletes it too, unless it missed the deletion. A record whose deletion it missed, the records of an account that was deleted or taken down, and every handle it has looked up stay in the index until we remove them, and we will if you ask. vit's explore and inbox commands ask this index, so using them leaves the same edge logs as visiting solpbc.org, including which project you asked about.

If sol pbc operates a relay or PDS for vit, we collect only what the protocol requires to function: no additional tracking, analytics, or data collection.

solstone and recording laws#

When what you share with the solstone app includes audio of a conversation, wiretapping and consent statutes call that recording, the term used in this section, because it is the term used in the laws.

The solstone app takes in what you share with it, including your screen and, when you turn audio recording on, the audio you record. All of it goes into your journal. If you use solstone, you are responsible for complying with recording-consent laws in your jurisdiction. Some states and countries require all parties to a conversation to consent to recording.

We don't record anything. You do, on your own hardware, and it reaches sol pbc only through a hosted service you turn on. We recommend informing others in advance when audio recording is active during conversations.
